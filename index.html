Does Going Viral Actually Move Stocks?

We analyzed three of the biggest denim celebrity campaigns of 2024–25. Here's what the data actually says about virality, attention, and shareholder value.

T4
Gurleen Virk, Aaliyah Brown, Elizabeth Diaz, Barbara Chen Team 4 · University of Illinois Urbana-Champaign

Every brand wants a cultural moment. Gap partnered with K-pop girl group KATSEYE for a music-driven Gen-Z campaign. Levi's tapped Beyoncé at the height of her Cowboy Carter era. American Eagle cast Sydney Sweeney in a campaign built entirely around a pun. Each launch generated press, social buzz, and millions of YouTube views. But did any of them actually make the stock go up?

That's the question we set out to answer in our BADM 534 final project. Using publicly available data — stock prices, Google Trends, YouTube engagement metrics, and over 3,000 user comments — we built a framework to measure what we call campaign attention and test whether it translates into measurable financial performance.

"Likes don't pay dividends. But search volume might be telling us something the market hasn't priced in yet."

The Three Campaigns

We selected these three campaigns because they're comparable in scale, all involve publicly traded fashion brands, and each has a distinct celebrity with a measurable online presence — making them ideal for a cross-campaign comparison.

How We Measured It

We defined campaign attention as three separate proxies, analyzed independently rather than collapsed into a single score:

Artist Attention — Google Trends search index for the celebrity name, ±12 weeks around launch.
Brand Attention — Google Trends search index for the brand name, same window.
Engagement Quality — YouTube channel monthly views + VADER sentiment scores from 1,000 comments per campaign.

For financial performance, we used Cumulative Abnormal Return (CAR) — the stock's return minus what the S&P 500 returned over the same ±30 day period. This isolates the campaign's impact from broader market movements.

Sentiment Findings

The AEO Paradox: Positive Comments, Weak Returns

We analyzed 1,000 YouTube comments per campaign using VADER, a natural language processing tool that scores text on a scale from −1 (negative) to +1 (positive). All three campaigns generated positive sentiment overall. But the rankings produced a counterintuitive result.

American Eagle had the highest percentage of positive comments of the three — 48.3% — but ended up with the weakest relative stock outcome. This is what we call the AEO Paradox, and it's the most analytically interesting finding in our dataset. High comment sentiment alone doesn't predict financial performance. Reach matters. Brand alignment matters. Sentiment without scale isn't enough.

Stock Findings

All Three Trended Positive. None Were Statistically Significant.

Here's where things get honest. We calculated CAR (Cumulative Abnormal Return) for each brand over a ±30 day window around campaign launch, comparing daily stock returns against the S&P 500 as a benchmark.

Cumulative Abnormal Return ±30 Days (vs. S&P 500)
Amer. Eagle

Gap

Levi's
+0.080

AEO's high CAR (+0.271) looks dramatic on paper — but its p-value was 0.704, far from the 0.05 threshold for statistical significance. All three campaigns had p-values between 0.68 and 0.78. None reached significance. This isn't a failure of our study — it's consistent with what economists call the Efficient Markets Hypothesis: markets price in publicly available information quickly, so campaign announcements don't produce sustained, abnormal returns.

What AEO's high CAR actually reflects is market volatility in AEO's stock during that period — not the Sydney Sweeney campaign. Which is exactly why stock price alone can't tell you if a campaign worked.

Regression Analysis

Our Causal Test: Difference-in-Differences

To go beyond correlation and attempt a causal estimate of campaign impact, we ran a Difference-in-Differences (DiD) regression. This method compares how treated brands (the ones that ran campaigns) performed relative to matched control brands in the same sector that didn't run a campaign in the same window.

Difference-in-Differences Model

Return = β₀ + β₁(Post) + β₂(Treated) + β₃(Post × Treated) + ε

Treated brands: Gap, Levi's, American Eagle (ran campaigns)

Control brands: Urban Outfitters, Kontoor Brands, Abercrombie (sector peers, no campaign)

Sample: n = 340 daily return observations · ±45 days per brand pair

β₃ (Post × Treated) is our DiD estimator — the causal effect of the campaign above and beyond what sector peers did in the same period.

Result: Campaign Effect (DiD) = +0.0018 · p = 0.944 · Not significant.
Campaigns produced no measurable return above sector peers. Markets are efficient.

A null result from a DiD isn't a failed study — it's an honest answer to a specific question. Celebrity campaigns, at least at this scale and over a 30-day window, don't produce statistically detectable abnormal returns above what comparable brands were doing anyway. The efficient markets hypothesis holds.

Key Takeaways

What We Actually Learned

01
Campaigns reliably drive attention — scale varies enormously

Every launch produced a measurable search and YouTube spike. But Gap's music-driven approach generated 8× more YouTube engagement than American Eagle's influencer campaign. The type of celebrity and campaign format matters for reach.

02
Brand search matters more than artist search for stock response

Beyoncé peaked at 100 on Google Trends — but that's people searching for Beyoncé. Gap's brand search index was higher than AEO's and directionally correlated with its stronger CAR. The campaign needs to pull attention to the brand, not just the celebrity.

03
Sentiment explains the gap between visibility and financial outcome

AEO's high view count and high positive comment percentage didn't translate. Reach without brand alignment — where consumers connect the celebrity to the brand, not just enjoy the celebrity — appears to limit financial impact.

04
Markets are efficient — campaigns don't produce immediate stock gains

None of our three campaigns produced statistically significant abnormal returns, and our DiD confirms no causal effect above sector peers. This doesn't mean campaigns don't matter — it means their financial impact likely shows up in longer-term metrics like brand equity, customer acquisition, and revenue, which fall outside a 30-day event window.

"Brands should evaluate campaigns using a combined dashboard — search index + video reach + sentiment + CAR. No single metric tells the full story."

Limitations & What Comes Next

We're the first to acknowledge what our study can't do. Three campaigns is enough to identify patterns — it's not enough to draw firm conclusions. Stock prices move for dozens of reasons beyond any single marketing event. Our sentiment model (VADER) was trained on general social media text, not specifically on fashion brand commentary. And we only studied campaigns that were already considered viral — there's no comparison group of quieter campaigns that didn't generate buzz.

The most natural extension would be expanding this framework to 20–30 campaigns across multiple years and industries. With that sample size, the regression becomes genuinely predictive rather than descriptive. A brand dashboard that tracks brand search index, YouTube engagement velocity, VADER sentiment, and CAR in real time — the kind of tool that would let a marketing team see early signals of whether a campaign is actually building financial value — is entirely buildable from publicly available data.

The data is out there. The methodology works. It just needs more campaigns.
