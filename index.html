Do Celebrity Campaigns Actually Move Stocks?

We analyzed celebrity fashion campaigns from Gap, Levi's, and American Eagle using Google Trends, YouTube engagement, sentiment analysis, and stock market event studies to test whether viral attention translates into measurable financial performance.

Fashion brands increasingly partner with celebrities and musicians to capture cultural relevance, reach Gen-Z audiences, and drive engagement online. According to Billboard, music artist partnerships now represent 42% of influencer marketing spend in entertainment, up from 28% in 2020.

But one major question remains: does consumer attention actually translate into financial performance?

"Campaigns consistently drove attention spikes — but stock market reactions were slower, weaker, and far less consistent."

Research Question

Our project focused on whether celebrity campaigns create measurable increases in consumer attention and whether that attention impacts stock performance around campaign launches.

We tested this relationship using:

  • Google Trends search data
  • YouTube engagement metrics
  • YouTube sentiment analysis
  • Stock market event studies using CAR (Cumulative Abnormal Return)

How We Measured Attention

Our framework connected campaign launch activity to consumer attention and then to financial performance. We triangulated multiple data sources so that no single metric determined our conclusions.

We defined campaign attention using three measurable proxies:

Google
Search interest surrounding campaign launches
YouTube
Campaign engagement and channel visibility
NLP
Sentiment analysis from campaign comments

For financial performance, we used an event study framework comparing stock returns against the S&P 500 benchmark over a ±30 day event window. Abnormal return was computed as the daily stock return minus the corresponding market return, and cumulative abnormal return (CAR) was calculated by summing abnormal returns across the event window.

One limitation of this approach is that the S&P 500 may still include firms with similar market exposure or correlated campaign effects. A cleaner counterfactual for future work would be a peer-firm portfolio excluding treated brands and firms running overlapping celebrity campaigns.

This comparison highlights one of the central findings of the project: attention type matters more than attention volume alone. Celebrity visibility, brand visibility, engagement reach, and sentiment do not necessarily move together.

One key insight from our analysis was that celebrity attention does not always convert into equivalent brand attention.

Stock Market Results

To test whether attention translated into financial performance, we calculated cumulative abnormal returns (CAR) around campaign launches.

Brand CAR ±30d P-Value Significant?
American Eagle +0.271 0.704 No
Gap +0.091 0.686 No
Levi's +0.080 0.781 No

Although all three campaigns showed positive CAR trends, none of the results were statistically significant. The results should therefore be interpreted as directionally positive rather than conclusive evidence of causal financial impact.

American Eagle produced the largest abnormal return, but our analysis suggests that this reflected broader stock volatility rather than direct campaign success.

"Consumer attention does not consistently translate into measurable financial outcomes."

Key Takeaways

1. Celebrity campaigns clearly drive attention.

Every campaign generated measurable increases in search activity and online engagement immediately after launch.

2. Reach and sentiment are different metrics.

American Eagle had the highest positive sentiment, while Gap generated the strongest overall reach.

3. Stock market reactions were weaker than attention spikes.

Campaign-related stock movement existed, but abnormal returns were inconsistent across brands and statistically insignificant in our tests. The evidence suggests that campaigns may contribute to short-term momentum, but the effects were underpowered in our sample.

4. Attention alone is not enough.

Celebrity visibility may strengthen brand awareness, but measurable financial impact likely depends on broader business fundamentals and long-term consumer behavior.

Limitations

Several limitations are important to acknowledge. First, our event study used the S&P 500 as the market benchmark, which may not represent a perfect control group because treated firms and related retail firms may still influence the index.

Second, celebrity campaigns occur alongside many other company and market events, making it difficult to isolate purely campaign-driven stock effects.

Finally, while attention metrics reacted immediately to campaign launches, stock markets may require longer horizons and larger datasets before meaningful financial effects become statistically detectable.

Final Thoughts

Our analysis highlights the growing gap between digital attention and financial outcomes. Brands can dominate social conversation without generating immediate shareholder value.

As celebrity partnerships continue growing across fashion and entertainment, understanding the difference between visibility, engagement, and actual financial performance becomes increasingly important.
